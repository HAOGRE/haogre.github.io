When Pangpang nailed up the sign at the entrance to the river valley, he made sure ONE TONNE was written extra large.

Over one tonne, sound the alarm.

Under one tonne, just keep going.

Their new world had a strange creature mod installed. You could even measure how much the cows weighed. They'd followed a recipe to build this weight detector: redstone wiring buried under the road, connected to a panel that displayed the weight, then to a bell. Steve was in charge of testing the machine. Alex was in charge of checking the numbers. Pangpang was in charge of explaining why the machine needed a chest full of bread beside it.

“You get hungry waiting for big cows,” he said.

A few days earlier, they'd come across a three-tonne cow, then a two-tonne one. They had plenty of meat in storage now, but Pangpang still wanted to know just how big the cows hiding in that valley could get.

On the first day, two sheep went past.

On the second day, three sheep went past.

On the third day, Pangpang began to suspect the sheep were working shifts.

That evening, the bell rang.

It only got halfway through.

“Clang—crack.”

Steve had barely stood up when the display fell off its wooden frame. The redstone lamps went out one by one. The bell lay crooked in the mud, and the ONE TONNE sign slowly toppled over, covering half its opening.

“It's here!” Pangpang grabbed his shield. “This one's got to be huge!”

They rushed to the entrance of the valley.

A cow was standing there.

It wasn't particularly big. Much smaller than the others they'd seen, at least.

Its head was down, and it was concentrating on a tuft of grass beside the sign.

Pangpang waited a moment with his shield raised, then peered behind the cow.

“Where's the big one?”

Alex picked up the panel. The screen was cracked, but the last reading was still there.

231.

She wiped the dirt off the unit to the right.

Tonnes.

Steve looked at the cow. Pangpang looked at the panel. Then they switched places and looked again.

The number hadn't changed. The cow hadn't got any bigger.

“The machine's broken,” Pangpang said.

“It certainly is now.” Alex handed him the snapped support. “That's what it said before it broke.”

Steve took a couple of steps closer. The cow raised its head, sniffed the wheat in his hand, and moved one hoof forward.

Under that hoof, the detector's last wooden plank split.

Pangpang slowly lowered his shield.

“All right. The machine has a point.”

The cow finished the wheat and nudged its nose into Steve's empty palm, apparently hoping for more.

Steve stroked its forehead.

“Let's take it home.”

Alex pointed at the detector on the ground. “Tell us how first.”

## The Boat Objected First

Pangpang thought the river would be the quickest way home.

Their camp was just downstream. No hills to cross, no need to use the old wooden bridge that hadn't been repaired in ages. Put the cow in the boat, float downstream, get it out at home. Nothing about the plan sounded like it would cost them anything.

Steve used wheat to lead the cow to the bank. Pangpang held the boat steady while Alex stood upstream with the rope.

The cow was very cooperative.

It put just one front hoof inside.

The bottom of the boat met the riverbed.

Pangpang looked down at the water that was suddenly up to his knees, let go of the boat, and took two steps back.

“It can dive.”

“The boat?” Steve asked.

“It can now.”

The cow withdrew its hoof. Half the boat floated to the surface. The other half went downstream.

Alex wrote in the ledger: One boat lost.

Pangpang leaned over. “It wasn't a total loss. We established that one method doesn't work.”

Alex added: Established that one method doesn't work. Cost: one boat.

They decided to go overland.

When they reached the wooden bridge, Steve had just raised the wheat when Alex put out a hand to stop him.

She sent both of them back onto the bank, then shut the gate at the entrance to the bridge.

Pangpang looked at the cow, then at the bridge.

“Maybe this bridge is sturdier.”

Alex set the remaining half of the boat at his feet.

Pangpang nodded. “A detour has its own scenery.”

## Building a Road for One Cow

In the end, they laid stone along the riverbank.

Steve filled in holes. Alex studied the map for the route with the gentlest slopes. Pangpang packed cobblestone into the low spots. In this world, a solid stone road could still support the cow. The planks, the boat, and their makeshift supports had already made their positions clear.

The cow followed Steve patiently.

When they stopped, it stopped. When they laid another stretch, it walked along it. Occasionally, when Steve forgot to look back, it gave a quiet moo to remind him that the wheat hadn't been handed out yet.

On his third trip hauling stone, Pangpang sat down beside the road to catch his breath.

“I used to think having a pet just meant feeding it.”

“And cleaning up where it lives,” Alex said as she passed.

Pangpang looked back at the long road they'd just laid.

“Ours wants transport infrastructure too.”

By the time they were nearly back at camp, the sun had dropped behind the trees. The cow stopped at the foot of the last slope and refused to go any farther.

Steve offered it wheat. It lowered its head, ate, and stayed where it was.

Pangpang walked around to inspect the area, then suddenly crouched down.

A short length of wooden plank was showing through the slope. It was part of the roof they'd built over their underground storeroom. Only a thin layer of dirt covered it, making it look just like the ground around it.

One more step, and the cow could have taken a tour of the storeroom.

Through the ceiling.

All three of them put their wheat away.

They added a bend to the path.

## Steve's New Pet

There were three steps at the entrance to camp.

The cow followed Steve to the first one and was about to lift a hoof when all three shouted, “Wait!”

It stopped.

Alex went to fetch stone. Steve cleared a patch of ground beside the house. Pangpang turned over the broken sign from the valley entrance and nailed it up again.

This time, he wrote:

> Two hundred and thirty-one tonnes.
>
> No going upstairs.

“It can't read,” Alex said.

“It's for Steve.”

They built a shelter beside the house, laid a stone floor, and put down a bundle of wheat. The cow walked in, nosed the food, and quietly lay down.

The three of them stood nearby and waited a moment.

The ground didn't crack.

Pangpang finally sat on the steps and tipped the bread out of his backpack. After a whole afternoon's work, he had three pieces left.

“What's its name?” he asked.

Steve, who was giving the cow more wheat, thought for a moment.

“Feather.”

Alex's pen stopped over the ledger.

Pangpang looked at the NO GOING UPSTAIRS sign and forgot to swallow his mouthful of bread.

“When you name things,” he said, “you show absolutely no respect for the losses.”

The cow lifted its head and gave a soft moo.

Steve turned to go upstairs for a blanket. The cow immediately stood up, ready to follow.

Pangpang didn't even have time to put his bread down. He grabbed the sign and rushed to the foot of the steps.

“Feather!”

He'd never put so much weight behind that name.
