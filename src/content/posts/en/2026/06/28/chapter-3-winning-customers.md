> “Buying AI for a company is like giving your grandmother an iPhone—she wants to use it, but she needs you to set it up.”
> — a16z (Andreessen Horowitz)

## 3.1 Choose Your Lighthouse Customers

The first lesson in acquiring users for an internet product is to choose your early adopters: 100 users who love you are worth more than 10,000 who think you are merely fine. The FDE equivalent is the lighthouse customer—a customer who brings revenue, but also sends a signal to the entire industry.

A lighthouse’s strategic value is amplified under the FDE model for three reasons.

First, a lighthouse is your strongest sales asset. Enterprise buying chains are long and risk-averse; peer endorsement is the shortest path to persuasion. Harvey’s origin story is a textbook example. Its first publicly announced major customer, in February 2023, was Allen & Overy, a global top-tier law firm with 3,500 lawyers across 43 offices. Once that lighthouse was lit, major customers such as PwC and Gibson Dunn followed. In law, pedigree matters: winning Allen & Overy effectively opened the door to the entire top-tier law-firm market. Palantir’s early history follows the same pattern. The CIA was its most demanding and most credible customer; trust from the intelligence community later became the key to Wall Street, manufacturing, and government markets.

Harvey’s process of lighting that lighthouse was itself an FDE lesson. In November 2022, Allen & Overy formed a dedicated Market Innovation Group, led by partner David Wakeling, and began quietly testing the product of a then little-known startup. Notice how they validated it: not through a demo, but through firm-wide real-world use. By the end of the pilot, 3,500 lawyers had asked the system roughly 40,000 real work questions across 250 practice areas and 50 languages. Wakeling’s conclusion became widely quoted in legal circles: “I’ve been doing legal tech for 15 years, and I’ve never seen anything this game-changing.” Another partner demonstrated a concrete use case to the press: asking the system to prepare a ten-page briefing for a US client on “how to open a bank in Luxembourg.” “It actually did it.” (See Appendix C.)

Second, the lighthouse determines your product DNA. In the FDE model, lessons from the field flow back into the product—which means your first ten customers effectively help shape it. Choose the wrong lighthouse, and the product gets pulled toward an idiosyncratic dead end. This is what a16z’s first piece of advice to startups, “sell smart,” is really about: a young company cannot satisfy everyone. Choose an ideal customer profile with enough commonality in its systems and use cases that learning accumulates from one engagement to the next instead of cancelling itself out.

Third, lighthouse quality matters an order of magnitude more than quantity. Your capacity is inherently scarce: an elite team can deeply serve only a handful of customers at once. Choosing the wrong one does not mean “missing one sale”; it can trap a top team in a swamp for six months. So check the three high-risk signals from Chapter 2—no business owner, refusal to provide real data, and cosmic-scale requirements—and add one lighthouse-specific test: agree early on whether the customer will stand up publicly after success—co-publish a case study, speak at industry events, or host visits from your prospects. A lighthouse unwilling to speak for you publicly is worth at least half as much.

### Beware the “Requirement Locust”

Internet products have to beware of “product locusts”: early users who swarm in, use the product, leave, and mislead its direction. The FDE equivalent is the “requirement locust”: a customer with ample budget and endless demands who drains your team without producing any compounding value. Three traits identify them: their requests clearly diverge from your strategy and cannot become reusable capability; they treat FDE as cheap outsourced labor, assigning tasks by headcount instead of aligning on outcomes; and their internal politics consume so much energy that your main job becomes helping one department prove another wrong. Saying no is difficult because their contracts are often tempting. But remember Barry’s accounting: a bad pilot burns not only immediate cost, but also your team’s time and morale—and the product compounding that could have grown around the right customer.

## 3.2 Start with the Dumbest Things

YC has an old maxim: “Do things that don’t scale.” The founders of the earliest home-rental platforms went door to door photographing hosts’ homes. The founders of Stripe installed software for users on the spot.

Asked on a podcast how FDE relates to this maxim, McGrew put it precisely: **the FDE model is doing things that don’t scale, at scale**.

That sentence captures the philosophy of FDE acquisition. In the enterprise market, there is no scalable shortcut to trust—only unglamorous work. It has three layers.

First: be there in person. The final item on a16z’s checklist for frontline deployment teams is just four words: show up personally. The reason is “a cliché, but clichés become clichés because they are true.” Being present improves sales and multiplies your odds of success when mapping internal power structures and helping a new tool get adopted. Enterprise trust is priced in meetings and shared experiences. Remote meetings create familiarity; trust is built by fighting side by side.

Second: get your hands dirty. OpenAI FDEs worked alongside agronomists in Iowa fields. Palantir engineers spent weeks on oil rigs and aircraft assembly lines. Harvey FDEs ran adoption demos with partners one by one inside law firms. None of this is “efficient,” but every instance produces something remote communication cannot: firsthand understanding of the customer’s circumstances. That lived experience becomes solution quality. Only after walking through muddy fields do you understand why a seemingly perfect mobile interface is unusable in direct sunlight.

Third: be a servant before you become a teacher. A common early mistake is entering with the attitude, “We are here to save you.” Get the posture wrong and every channel of information closes. Start with humble service: fix a data issue for an analyst, complete an interface document for IT, or automate a weekly report for a business team. These “dumb” tasks buy three strategic assets: a real map of the organization, the truth about its data environment, and most importantly, authentication as “one of us.” Once that identity is established, your words begin to carry weight.

The destination of dumb work is not to remain dumb forever. Chapter 7 will explain how to turn these practices into repeatable playbooks—but before scaling, you must first find, in the mud, a path worth copying.

## 3.3 The Trust Dividend: Mining Lighthouse Endorsements

Enterprise procurement is a market with extreme information asymmetry and extremely high failure costs. Decision-makers rank information sharply: vendor marketing ranks below analyst reports; analyst reports below public peer case studies; public case studies below private peer recommendations. The final tier—“Someone I know used them and says they really work”—crushes every other channel in conversion. The FDE model is precisely the best machine for producing private-recommendation material. You are not delivering a software license; you are creating the sentence a customer executive says at a dinner with peers: “They really understand our business.”

![Image 1](/uploads/2026/06/28/chapter-3-winning-customers/ch4_1_1785725155563.jpg)

Mine this deposit at three levels.

First: turn delivery into a story worth telling. An executive will spread your story only if it makes them look good in front of peers. The result needs three narrative elements: a concrete number, a concrete person, and a concrete contrast. At the end of delivery, prepare the internal champion with a one-page summary, three charts, and a version that can be told in 30 seconds. The precision of your material determines whether the story survives retelling.

Second: turn customer success into social currency. After Walgreens deployed to 4,000 stores through a boot-camp model over eight months, it became a star at industry conferences. J.D. Power went further: after attending a Palantir boot camp as a customer, it began running boot camps for its own customers. (See Appendix C.) This is the ideal state: the customer presents your methodology as its own industry leadership. The endorsement evolves from “thank you” to “we are proud to use you.”

Third: beware endorsement backlash. Enterprise markets have long memories. A failed delivery for a high-profile customer travels much faster than ten successful ones, because failure makes a better story. That is why Chapter 2’s refusal mechanism and Chapter 4’s activation discipline matter so much: the fatal weakness of a lighthouse strategy is not failing to find a lighthouse, but letting it go dark in your hands.

## 3.4 Use Data to Understand the Customer’s House

Before and after entering a customer, FDE teams should conduct “entry diligence”: before writing the first line of code, use a structured process to understand the customer’s entire situation. A complete diligence checklist contains five maps.

- **Data map:** data sources, owners, quality, access controls, and hidden data such as spreadsheets kept by longtime employees, reports circulated only by email, and paper ledgers. The question is not what exists, but what people trust.
- **Process map:** how the target business process really operates—not the documented version, but the version revealed by shadowing the work. Mark the steps with the greatest time cost, the highest cost of error, and the strongest emotions.
- **Organization map:** who initiates, pays, uses, vetoes, and holds informal power. Find the “knowledge hubs”: people without senior titles whom everyone consults when practical problems arise.
- **Systems map:** the real technical environment, including systems and interfaces, security and compliance boundaries, and change-management procedures.
- **Political map:** whose power is threatened, whose work may appear redundant, and whose fears stand behind resistance. Give those people a new path forward, not a dead end.

Only when all five maps are complete have you truly entered the customer. The work usually takes one to two weeks, with “Echo” and “Delta” working separately and reconciling daily. It looks like pure cost, but the accounting is simple: two weeks of diligence can save three months of running hard on the wrong battlefield.

## 3.5 Technical Content Marketing: A Compounding Trust Engine

Content marketing is a classic acquisition weapon for internet companies. For an FDE company, it has a distinct role: **not chasing traffic, but preselling professional trust**.

Enterprise customers do not usually discover you through an ad. They encounter a hard problem, search and ask around, find a company that has written about it with unusual expertise, and conclude: “They understand this. We should talk to them.” Content acts as qualification at the very front of the buying chain.

FDE content follows three rules:

- **Write from the trenches, not the showroom.** Explain how hard the problem was and how you got through it, rather than how powerful the product is.
- **Open-source the methodology and earn the right to be cited.** When customers begin using your framework to ask questions, you move from vendor to question-setter.
- **Make the customer’s internal champion the hero.** Your team is the partner who helped them succeed. Giving the champion a stage invites similar people at the next customer to step forward.

## 3.6 Getting Through Procurement, Legal, and Security Review

The gates of the enterprise market are procurement, legal, and security review committees. Treat them as part of delivery and you can turn them into an advantage.

Procurement: provide phased pricing, comparable market benchmarks, and measurable outcome commitments. Outcome-based pricing makes it clear what the buyer receives.

Legal: productize the data-processing agreement. Clarify whether data is used for training, where it is stored, who can access it, who bears liability for incidents, and how erroneous AI output is handled. **Legal trust is designed, not negotiated.**

Security review: maintain a proactive security white paper covering deployment architecture, data flows, encryption and access controls, compliance certifications, and standard answers to questions raised in past reviews. Response speed is itself a visible signal of security maturity.

## 3.7 Ecosystem Bundling: Stand on the Shoulders of Channels

The FDE ecosystem strategy relies on three kinds of partners: cloud providers and major platforms, consulting firms and systems integrators, and your customer’s customers.

Cloud providers can place you inside enterprise procurement lists, while their marketplaces let customers spend existing cloud commitments on your service. Consulting firms and integrators bring customer relationships and industry depth, though they may also consume your delivery layer under the banner of partnership. The deepest bundle is embedding yourself in the customer’s value chain: design delivery so the customer can use your solution to serve its own customers. Then you become part of the customer’s business model rather than merely its vendor.

## 3.8 Scheduling: Who Goes First Is Strategy

Your capacity will always be smaller than demand, so “who goes first” becomes a strategic tool.

First, schedule by strategic value, not contract size. The matrix has two dimensions: lighthouse value and learning value. Customers high on both deserve priority.

Second, productize the wait. While a customer is queued, provide a data-preparation checklist, organizational-readiness advice, and lightweight remote diagnosis. A well-managed waiting period becomes part of delivery quality.

Third, never promise parallel work beyond capacity. It is better to sell a full schedule for next quarter than to exhaust the team across three customers. The busier the business becomes, the more tempting overbooking is—and the greater the cost when it breaks delivery.

## 3.9 Make the Brand Ubiquitous with Open Source and Demos

Turn what you do well into open-source tools and demos so target customers’ engineers can use them first. Executives initiate enterprise purchases, but engineers often hold technical veto power. Early technical adoption plants a vote of trust inside the buying chain.

![Image 3](/uploads/2026/06/28/chapter-3-winning-customers/ch4_3_1785725175383.jpg)

There are three typical vehicles: open-source tools and components, self-serve technical showcases, and a sustained presence in engineering communities. The spread of a standard is the accumulation of trust. A quick-start package that lets an engineer connect sample data and get running in an afternoon is the best sales engineer you can build. Recruiting and acquisition converge here: the best candidate pool and the best customer leads often come from the same community.

## 3.10 Writing Proposals and Validation Plans

The most valuable documents in the enterprise market are proposals and proof-of-concept plans. Good FDE proposals follow a strict inverted pyramid:

1. **Business outcome:** state in the customer’s language what they will gain.
2. **Validation path:** specify acceptance metrics, measurement methods, baselines, and exit criteria.
3. **Delivery method:** explain the technical approach, milestones, decisions, and customer commitments.
4. **Risks and mitigations:** make clear how the project could fail and what you will do about it.

A proof-of-concept plan must also define the maximum duration, acceptance metrics, and the two possible next steps: graduation or separation. A vague validation plan is permission to open another proof-of-concept graveyard.

## 3.11 From On-Site to Remote: The Boundary of Hybrid Delivery

FDE has evolved from fully on-site work toward a hybrid model: remote by default, with on-site presence at critical moments. The moments that usually require physical presence are the beginning of the relationship, high-intensity co-creation, and politically sensitive periods. Deep coding, documentation, and routine iteration are often better done remotely.

Hybrid delivery improves both cost structure and talent sustainability. But the boundary is clear: **an FDE engagement with no face-to-face contact is a contradiction**. Remote work can maintain trust and context; those must first be created in the physical world.

## 3.12 Expanding Overseas

In 2026, FDE’s international expansion has two distinct patterns.

US companies provide the global example: OpenAI’s FDE hiring footprint spans San Francisco, New York, Seattle, London, Dublin, Munich, Paris, Zurich, Tokyo, Singapore, Sydney, and Abu Dhabi. The geography maps the world’s enterprise AI purchasing power.

For Chinese companies, the logic is different. The old problem was product DNA: domestic vendors were accustomed to highly customized project work, while overseas markets demanded standardization and productization. FDE reverses the perspective. Chinese engineers’ delivery culture—hardworking, responsive, full-stack, and comfortable solving everything on-site—fits FDE unusually well. The real question is whether the platform beneath them is strong enough.

The advantage of going abroad is that the lighthouse-to-endorsement logic works globally. The constraint is that FDE is deeply localized: language, time zones, compliance, and local trust networks all require local teams. The cost structure is therefore inherently higher than that of online software. Establish a remote-deliverable product first, then build local delivery teams in priority markets one country at a time.

The twelve lessons in this chapter boil down to one sentence: **winning customers means becoming the trustworthy exception in a market that has been disappointed countless times**. Signing the contract is only the entry ticket. The real battle begins afterward: making the system come alive and getting people to use it.
