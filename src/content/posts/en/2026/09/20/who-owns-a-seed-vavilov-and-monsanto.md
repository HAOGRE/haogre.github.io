I recently learned about Monsanto. Following that thread, I also learned a little about the Vavilov Institute.

Both names have to do with seeds, but they evoke very different feelings. Monsanto brings to mind commercial breeding, patents, and the power of agricultural companies. The most moving story associated with the Vavilov Institute comes from a war.

During the siege of Leningrad in World War II, the institute's staff protected its germplasm collection while facing starvation. Some died of hunger; the seeds survived. [^siege]

People protecting seeds with their lives on one side, patents restricting their use on the other. It is an emotionally powerful contrast, and I understand the reaction it invites. But thinking about it further brings other needs into view. Seeds must be conserved and improved. Breeding needs investment, and farmers must plant another season.

Putting these two names together makes me wonder: **who owns a seed, and whom do we want to decide its future?**

## Leave future choices open

The Vavilov Institute conserves and studies plant genetic resources. Nikolai Vavilov traveled widely to collect crop material and investigate the origins and diversity of cultivated plants. Different landraces, developed varieties, and wild relatives can carry different genetic characteristics, providing material for later breeding for disease resistance, cold tolerance, or drought tolerance. [^conservation]

What interests me most is that the people conserving these resources do not know which accession will eventually prove useful.

A local variety with low yields and a small market today may be difficult to sell at a good price. It may nevertheless carry a trait that future agriculture needs. Being unpopular now does not make it useless later. But if we discard it first, we may not be able to find it when we need it.

That is why I value genebanks. They let us postpone the question of how much money something can earn and keep the option open.

It is hard to sustain this work entirely through immediate commercial returns. When material is conserved, nobody can say which company or region will benefit decades later. If every investment must lead to a quick, identifiable payoff, resources with unknown uses will struggle for priority.

I am willing to pay for that public work. Even if many accessions are never used, conserving them need not have been a waste. We pay to have more possible answers when a future problem arrives.

This takes continuing investment. Seeds need suitable storage conditions, viability testing, and regeneration when necessary. Some material requires field collections, in vitro culture, or cryopreservation. Building a storage facility completes only part of the job. [^conservation]

## Breeding needs a return, and farmers need another crop

At this point, it is easy to cast Monsanto as the opposite. But I think the contributions and costs of commercial breeding deserve a fair accounting too.

Conserved material still needs research, selection, and trials before it becomes a variety that farmers want to grow. A company invests money and takes the risk of failure. There is a reasonable case for rewarding success.

The complication is that seeds are products that reproduce.

In 2013, *Bowman v. Monsanto* brought that problem before the U.S. Supreme Court. The Roundup Ready soybeans in the case tolerated glyphosate. Monsanto's license allowed growers to plant purchased seed for one season and consume or sell the resulting crop as a commodity, but not save it for replanting. [^bowman]

Farmer Vernon Bowman bought licensed seed each year for his first crop and followed the agreement. For his riskier late-season crop, he bought commodity soybeans from a grain elevator, where they were intended for food or feed, expecting many to carry the resistant trait. He planted them, applied glyphosate, and saved part of the harvest from surviving plants. He produced eight crops this way. [^bowman]

The Court unanimously ruled for Monsanto. It distinguished the soybeans Bowman bought from the additional soybeans he subsequently grew. An authorized purchase of the former did not confer permission to keep reproducing the patented invention. [^bowman]

Two distinctions matter here. These seeds could reproduce; the restriction came from patents and licensing. Bowman was also not sued because seeds happened to blow into his field. He deliberately planted, selected, and repeatedly saved them. Getting those facts wrong makes the rest of the discussion impossible.

The reasoning the Court upheld is understandable: if one purchase permits unlimited reproduction and sale of later generations, the developer's return could quickly erode. Yet seeds naturally reproduce, and farmers naturally need to plant again. Where the law draws that boundary directly affects who can continue using them.

**I support a return for breeders, and I want that return to leave room for subsequent research and farmers' choices.**

Acknowledging the cost of research does not make every restriction reasonable. Acknowledging farmers' need to save seed does not make new varieties move from laboratories to fields on their own. Both sides belong in the accounting.

## “Buy from someone else” requires someone else to buy from

If you dislike a contract, you can decline it. How much that advice is worth depends on what alternatives you have.

Table 1 of a 2023 USDA Economic Research Service report uses company shares from 2016 to 2017 to estimate the post-merger market in 2018 to 2020: Bayer and Corteva together account for about 72 percent of U.S. retail corn seed sales and 66 percent of soybean seed sales. These are historical estimates for particular U.S. crop markets, not a description of today's global seed market. [^usda]

The figures alone cannot establish that a particular price is unreasonable. They do justify serious questions. Are there enough alternative varieties suited to local conditions? Are the choices offered by different companies meaningfully different? How many permissions does a small company need before it can enter the market?

Bayer acquired Monsanto in 2018. The U.S. Department of Justice's proposed merger settlement required Bayer to divest approximately $9 billion in agricultural businesses and assets to BASF to address competition concerns. Whether a technology works and how much competition remains in its market are questions that need to be considered together. [^competition]

I want public germplasm conservation and public breeding to endure, and I want enough competition in the seed industry. They give farmers somewhere to turn after saying no to a particular contract.

There is another contribution that is easy to leave out. Before a seed enters a company's laboratory, it has already passed through generations of cultivation, selection, and conservation. The company that eventually commercializes a product contributes something. So do the farmers and communities that supplied material and accumulated local knowledge.

FAO's International Treaty on Plant Genetic Resources for Food and Agriculture recognizes those contributions. It addresses traditional knowledge, benefit sharing, and farmers' participation in decisions, and establishes an access and benefit-sharing system for a defined set of resources. It does not make all seeds freely available as global common property; rights concerning farm-saved seed remain subject to national law and other conditions. [^treaty]

I support the effort behind this: innovators can earn a return, while those who contributed material and knowledge earlier should also have a place in decisions and benefit sharing. Their work should not disappear from the final accounting simply because it happened long ago or was spread across many places.

## Protect the people doing the science too

The Vavilov Institute cannot simply be treated as a reassuring answer, either.

Vavilov himself suffered political persecution. Against the background of Soviet political support for Trofim Lysenko and attacks on genetics, he was arrested in 1940 and died in Saratov prison in 1943. He did not die guarding the collection with his colleagues during the siege. Those are separate histories. [^history]

That makes me think that placing seeds in public institutions is not enough. An institute can have a public mission while its scientists lose the freedom to question and work. Conserving material needs funding, people, and technical capacity. Studying it also needs institutions that can accommodate evidence and disagreement.

I respect the people who preserved the collection, but I do not want their sacrifice presented as the normal price of conservation. Good arrangements should make this work sustainable, without requiring people to give their lives to fill the gaps left by institutional failure and catastrophe.

Learning about these two names has not left me with an answer as simple as “research institutes good, companies bad.” What I want is someone conserving genetic diversity that has no immediate use, someone willing to invest in breeding, room for later researchers to continue the work, and affordable seeds that give farmers meaningful choices.

To conserve a seed is to leave an option open for the future. In judging today's seed institutions, we should also ask: decades from now, who will still have those options?
