Over the weekend, my wife and I took our son to a science fiction museum. We had a lovely time. Halfway through our visit, we came across a museum-themed ice cream bar that cost 18 yuan.

Judged purely as ice cream, it was obviously overpriced. You could buy something similar outside for two or three yuan. My wife and I both thought it was unnecessary, but our son stood there, unwilling to leave. He still wanted it.

In the end, we did not buy it. The adults controlled the money, so when we said no, that was that.

That evening, just before bed, I brought it up again. By then, I had begun to think that perhaps we should have bought it after all.

The 18 yuan was not just paying for something cold to eat. In that particular setting, the ice cream offered more than that.

It was made for the museum. Its shape and packaging were tied to the place, and once we left, we could not buy it anywhere else. For a child, the fact that it existed only there, at that particular moment, might have been what made it worth the price.

I asked him, “If you could choose again, would you still want that 18-yuan ice cream?”

He answered without hesitation. “Yes. I still think it was worth that much, because you can’t buy it anywhere else.”

Because you can’t buy it anywhere else. Once he said that, there was not much left for me to argue with.

He was right. It was worth it, and 18 yuan was hardly a serious expense for me. This did not need to become a lesson about values. I did not have to use the moment to teach him thrift, nor did I have to let an adult’s judgment overrule how he felt.

It is like a toy you desperately wanted as a child. Years later, when you earn your own money and finally buy it, it is only a toy. It is no longer the toy you wanted back then.

The ice cream was the same. Away from the museum and that particular day, it would have been nothing more than a two-or-three-yuan ice cream bar. What made it valuable was how much he wanted it at that moment, and the fact that it could only be bought there.

That day, I was calculating the difference between two yuan and eighteen. I was asking whether it was worth the money. My son was asking whether he would ever be able to get it anywhere else. We were never doing the same calculation.

Being a parent is more than meeting a child's basic needs. There are some things only a parent can give. The ice cream was not worth much money, but being willing to bring it back up that evening and talk it through is probably one of them.
