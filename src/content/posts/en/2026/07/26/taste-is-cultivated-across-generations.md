I saw two performances this weekend.

The first was a symphonic concert featuring music from *Black Myth: Wukong*. The tickets were certainly not cheap, but by the end, I felt they were worth it.

When music from a game is brought into a concert hall and performed in full by a live orchestra, it is no longer merely part of the game. It becomes a work capable of standing at center stage on its own. The force of the concert made me feel that what a game can carry has already traveled far beyond the game itself.

Today, I also saw the newly released animated film *The Eight Immortals*. Overall, I thought it was fairly good. It did not move me as deeply as the symphonic concert, but there was still something fascinating about seeing a story we have known since childhood remade as an animated film for a contemporary audience.

<small>Figure 01 | Old stories step onto new stages</small>

Taken together, the two experiences made me think about something: art does not appear out of nowhere, and neither does the ability to appreciate it.

For a work to come into being, it needs creators, but it also needs an audience. Some people must be willing to spend years learning their craft. Some must care enough to make the work well. And others must be willing to buy a ticket, enter the theater, and offer a genuine response. Of course, it is reasonable to ask whether a ticket costs too much. But when a performance is truly good, paying for it is also one way of helping more good work come into existence.

Whether someone can recognize what is good in a work often depends on what they have seen and heard before. A childhood spent encountering music, painting, theater, and film makes it easier, later in life, to tell what genuinely moves us from what is merely spectacle. The more we experience, the more gradually we form judgments of our own.

Nurturing this ability is not simply a matter of making a child learn an instrument or memorize facts about art. More importantly, it means allowing art to become a natural part of everyday life. Take a child to a performance or a concert, then talk afterward about what they liked and what they did not. Even “I didn't find it interesting” is more valuable than repeating the approved answer that something was “beautiful.” Perhaps this is where taste begins: first knowing what you like, then slowly coming to understand why.

![A father draws back a theater curtain as a child follows a slowly unfolding path toward the stage](/uploads/2026/07/26/审美是一代代养出来的/02-from-audience-to-stage.png)

<small>Figure 02 | The journey from the audience to the stage is a long one</small>

One generation creates; the next watches and learns to understand. From that audience, new creators eventually emerge. A child sitting in a theater today may simply think that an image is beautiful or that a piece of music feels powerful. Many years from now, some of those children may become painters, directors, or musicians. Others may simply become ordinary people who are willing to give art their full attention.

None of this can be rushed. It takes performance after performance and work after work. It also takes one generation leading the next, slowly, into the theater.

I went to two performances this weekend, but what stayed with me was a much slower thought: art must be nurtured from one generation to the next, and so must the people who appreciate it.
