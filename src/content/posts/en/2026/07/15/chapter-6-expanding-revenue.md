> “The FDE model is working when each successive customer requires less customization.”
> — Bob McGrew

## 6.1 The World of Free Validation

The internet economy turned “free” from a gimmick into a strategy. FDE is no exception, though the obstacle looks different here: **in the enterprise AI era, the most expensive form of free is a free proof of concept**.

Start with the scale of this free economy. Palantir’s AIP Bootcamps essentially turned free validation into a production line: customers bring real data, and in one to five days produce a deployable prototype, free or nearly free. Starting at fewer than a hundred sessions in 2022, the number doubled year after year, reaching nearly six sessions a day at its 2025 peak. At several top engineers working for days on each session, that represents tens of millions of dollars in annual free investment. Former Palantir engineer Barry put the early reality even more bluntly: “We burned millions of dollars on customer pilots. The margins on many projects were literally negative infinity because we did them for free.”

Why does free validation work? The numbers work across three ledgers.

The first ledger is customer acquisition. Traditional enterprise software acquires customers with large sales armies: travel, dinners, bids, and long negotiations. The money is both substantial and hard to control. Bootcamps use a different playbook: instead of persuading the customer, let the customer persuade themselves. An executive personally operating a system on their own data is worth more than a hundred slides. Palantir compressed its sales cycle from nine to twelve months to a matter of weeks, while U.S. commercial revenue grew 137% year over year in a quarter. Free Bootcamps are widely recognized as the main engine. **Free validation is not a cost; it exchanges sales expense for engineering expense—and engineering expense can compound into a product, while sales expense cannot**.

The second ledger is the power to price risk. McGrew’s advice is that early-stage startups should take on the risk themselves: “You pay us when it works.” The confidence comes from belief in the product, but also from a sober calculation: an enterprise customer’s greatest concern about a new vendor is “Can you actually do this?” Free validation dissolves that concern. Once doubt has dissolved, pricing power returns to you. The customer is no longer buying “a bet,” but “certainty they have personally verified.” Certainty can command a premium.

The third ledger is making failure valuable. Free validation inevitably produces failures—that is basic portfolio-investment logic. The difference lies in where failure goes. A failed traditional sale leaves behind travel receipts; a failed FDE-style validation leaves behind industry knowledge, reusable components, and evaluation data. As long as you establish the feedback loop described in Chapter 7, failed validations are still deposits into the company’s account.

But free validation has one fatal prerequisite: it must operate as a graduation system, not an indefinite residency system. That brings us to the next section.

## 6.2 The End of the Free Lunch

Free is the means, payment is the goal, and conversion design is existential. In the FDE world, converting validation into paid work is the decisive final step—and the point where the industry sees the most accidents. Most of the “proof-of-concept graveyards” mentioned throughout the earlier chapters are not caused by technical failure, but by **the failure to design an ending**.

The transition from free to paid requires five switches to be designed in advance.

- **Switch one:** Set graduation criteria before work begins. The principle emphasized in Chapter 2 becomes concrete here: when the validation project starts, put it in writing—the maximum duration, acceptance metrics, and the agreement to enter commercial negotiations once the criteria are met. Palantir’s Bootcamps take this to an extreme: on the schedule for days four and five, “demo” is followed immediately by “decision.” Conversion is not an event that happens afterward; it is a box on the calendar.

- **Switch two:** Make the boundaries of free explicit. The customer must know exactly when the free period ends, what it covers, and how anything beyond that will be priced. Ambiguous free boundaries create the expectation that free is normal. When billing begins, the customer does not feel that they are “starting to pay”; they feel that they have been “taken for a ride.” The amount may be identical, but different expectations produce radically different experiences.

- **Switch three:** Turn the internal champion into a salesperson. After validation succeeds, the person who actually knocks on the budget door is not your salesperson, but the customer-side champion who witnessed the value firsthand. Your job is to equip them: a one-page value report with numbers, comparisons, and colleague testimony; a Q&A pack for finance objections; and a clear statement of the opportunity cost of not continuing. The internal trust of an internal advocate is something an outsider can never fully reach.

![Image 1](/uploads/2026/07/15/chapter-6-expanding-revenue/ch2_3_1785725081346.jpg)

- **Switch four:** Plant the price anchor early. Talk about value during the free period: “This system freed up roughly 120 person-hours for you this month.” When the value narrative runs through the free period, the customer already has an anchor when the quote arrives. If the free period discusses only features and never value, the quote feels like a sudden shock.

- **Switch five:** Give non-conversion a dignified exit. Not every validation should become a paid contract; forced conversion creates poisonous contracts. For customers who do not meet the criteria or are not ready, offer a “pause and preserve” option: retain the data and configuration, agree on conditions for restarting, and maintain lightweight contact. The enterprise market is small. “Let’s talk next year” today often becomes a major deal the year after—provided you handle the goodbye professionally.

Taken together, the five switches say one thing: **turn “free to paid” from a perilous leap into a gradual slope**.

## 6.3 Outcome-Based Pricing: Customers Pay for What They Get

Advertising has an efficient playbook: track user behavior and target precisely. FDE pricing follows the same simple principle: **charge in proportion to the value the customer receives**.

The dominant pricing model in the online software era is per seat—customers pay for the right to use the software. That logic is eroding in the AI era. When one agent can do the work of ten people, per-seat pricing starts to look absurd—should customers pay for 0.1 of a seat? Outcome-based pricing is emerging instead. Sierra’s charge per “resolved conversation” is the clearest example: customers do not pay for software; they pay for problems being solved.

Outcome-based pricing has four levels of evolution. The higher the level, the closer it is to value—and the harder it is to execute.

Usage-based: charge by model units, calls, or volume processed. The advantage is clarity and measurability. The weakness is that it tracks cost rather than value: high usage may mean high value, or it may mean an inefficient system. Usage-based model APIs are the industry baseline, but application-layer companies rarely use them as their only pricing model.

Action-based: charge for “processing one return” or “generating one compliance report.” This is a step beyond usage because the pricing unit now has business meaning.

Result-based: charge for a “successfully resolved conversation” or a “recovered bad debt.” Sierra’s per-resolution pricing and some risk-control companies’ share of recovered losses belong here. The challenge is attribution. What counts as “resolved” requires a standard both sides accept. That is precisely the commercial use of the evaluation framework in Chapter 4: a technical evaluation system is also billing infrastructure.

Value sharing: take a share of the financial value created for the customer—cost saved, revenue recovered, or capacity released. This is the ultimate form of value alignment, and the hardest to execute. It requires access to the customer’s financial data, trust that can withstand business cycles, and exceptional measurement capabilities. For now, it appears only in some deeply integrated, high-value scenarios.

Companies that charge by results must be willing to publish those results. Sierra’s customer data makes for an interesting scorecard: property-management company Funnel Leasing, 94% self-service resolution; fintech company Ramp, 90%; mattress brand Casper, 74%, with customer satisfaction up by more than 20%; WW, roughly 70%, with satisfaction at 4.6 out of 5; even the lowest-performing customer reached 64%.

Third-party estimates have brought the price range into view as well: annual contracts starting at roughly $150,000, common first-year budgets of $200,000 to $350,000 including deployment fees, and large customers reaching the million-dollar-per-year range. Reportedly, a successful resolution is priced at $1 to $2. In other words, **every penny the customer pays corresponds to a problem that was actually solved**. Sierra can charge this way because its evaluation system can prove that the problem was solved. Pricing and evaluation are two sides of the same coin here. (See Appendix C for sources.)

There is one straightforward rule for choosing a pricing level: **the closer the pricing unit is to customer value, the higher your pricing ceiling—but the greater your measurement and trust costs**.

For readers in China, one realistic footnote is necessary: enterprise customers in China still have limited appetite for subscriptions. “Perpetual purchase plus implementation” and “payment upon project acceptance” remain the mainstream models. FDE pricing in China therefore often needs a hybrid approach: milestone-based delivery and acceptance, respecting project-based habits, with value metrics written into the acceptance criteria, introducing the logic of outcome-based pricing. Pure subscription is the ideal here; a hybrid model is the way to survive.

## 6.4 Deepening Existing Accounts: From One Department to a Network

![Image 2](/uploads/2026/07/15/chapter-6-expanding-revenue/ch3_1_1785725126077.jpg)

Enterprise markets follow an iron law: **the greatest revenue growth does not come from new customers, but from inside existing ones**. The industry measures this through net revenue retention. Strong FDE-driven companies routinely maintain NRR above 120%: without signing a single new deal, their existing revenue grows by 20%. Palantir’s commercial story is fundamentally a story of deepening existing accounts: from one intelligence team to an entire institution, from one factory to an entire group, from government departments to a commercial empire.

The industry has an apt phrase for this playbook: “land and expand.” The first five chapters cover landing; expansion has three directions.

- **Horizontally—from one team to adjacent teams:** If you built an intelligent ticketing system for customer service, the after-sales and technical-support departments next door are your easiest targets. The most persuasive evidence is inside the customer: the same company, the same data environment, and a colleague from the adjacent department speaking from experience. This is expansion with the least sales friction; almost no new trust needs to be built. Harvey’s expansion through law firms follows this rhythm: enter through one practice group, validate in real-world use for six months, then expand across the firm.

- **Vertically—from operators to decision-makers:** Initial projects usually serve frontline operators. Vertical expansion carries value upward through the chain: analytics and alerts for middle managers, decision dashboards for executives. The value is not only revenue but security. As Chapter 5 noted, a system loved only by frontline staff has no defender during budget season. A system visible to executives becomes part of the organization’s fixed assets.

- **In depth—from supporting tool to core process:** The deepest expansion turns a system from “a helpful tool” into “a process the business cannot do without”—from giving advice to executing business actions, from optional to part of the standard operating procedure. Every step brings greater responsibility and a higher trust threshold, but it also builds the deepest moat. Replacing a tool means changing software; replacing a system embedded in a workflow is surgery.

Two land-and-expand scorecards are worth comparing. Harvey started with one law firm, Latham & Watkins. By 2026, it had more than 100,000 lawyer users across 1,300 organizations, covering most of the Am Law 100, more than 500 in-house legal teams, and 50 asset-management firms in 60 countries. Annual recurring revenue rose from roughly $100 million in August 2025 to about $190 million in January 2026—nearly doubling in five months. An industry survey found that 68% of responding firms were already using Harvey’s agents in production, while heavy users saved an average of 11 hours per week. Its valuation jumped four times in a year: $3 billion, $5 billion, $8 billion, and $11 billion. (See Appendix C for sources.) Palantir’s 139% NRR scorecard was covered in Section 5.1. Together, the two tables show the ultimate form of deepening existing accounts: **new bookings come from marketing; growth comes from the installed base**.

All three directions share the same discipline: **expansion must be pulled by value, not pushed by sales targets**. The health framework from Chapter 5 has an offensive use here. Departments with deep usage and clear value are the next expansion targets. And when someone in the customer organization sees another team getting good results and proactively asks about it, that is the golden window. You are not selling; you are responding to demand.

## 6.5 How Anthropic and FIS Played the Financial-Services Card

This chapter includes a complete case study: one of the most representative partnerships in the 2026 enterprise AI market—Anthropic and financial-technology giant FIS building a financial-crime agent together. It demonstrates almost the entire methodology of this chapter.

First, the setup. FIS is a global giant in financial-technology infrastructure, serving the core systems of banks worldwide. In May 2026, FIS launched a financial-crime detection agent, with Bank of Montreal and Amalgamated Bank among its first customers. The agent compresses anti-money-laundering investigations from hours to minutes: it automatically assembles evidence across bank core systems, ranks cases by risk for investigators, and remains auditable and traceable throughout.

Now the playbook: four interlocking moves.

Move one: embed rather than deliver. Anthropic sent an applied-AI team and frontline deployment engineers to work directly inside FIS, designing the system alongside FIS experts. Note that FIS is not the end customer but a channel-level partner. Anthropic’s agent will enter the hundreds of banks behind FIS through FIS’s product. This is one deal and the gateway to a hundred more.

Move two: make knowledge transfer part of the pitch. The official announcement specifically says that the embedded team’s goals include transferring knowledge so FIS can independently build and expand more agents in the future. Put “teach the customer” into the contract. This is both a preemptive response to concerns about vendor lock-in—echoing the “vendor withdrawal reaction” in Section 5.1—and an advanced form of attachment: when the customer’s technology stack grows around your methodology, separation only becomes more expensive.

Move three: make auditability a product feature. In financial compliance, regulators require every decision to be replayable. Anthropic made “fully auditable and traceable” a core selling point of the agent, not an add-on. Compliance is not merely a constraint; it is differentiation. This offers a template for every regulated industry: **the compliance cost others see can become your reason for charging a premium**.

Move four: amplify through the ecosystem. On the same day the FIS case was announced, Anthropic also announced connectors and ready-to-use templates for financial services, along with more than a dozen similar partnerships. A single case was immediately abstracted into a repeatable product asset. Around the same time, the market also reported that Anthropic was forming an enterprise-AI services company with Blackstone, reportedly worth about $1.5 billion, positioning it directly against OpenAI’s deployment company. (See Appendix C for all sources above.)

Finally, the hidden card in the game: CIO vigilance. A technology publication aimed at enterprise IT leaders quoted a strategy executive at a consulting firm warning that “the most structural question in this model is who is paying for the cost of frontline deployment—this is what CIOs should ask, but most have not.” Gartner analysts, meanwhile, predicted that by 2028, 70% of enterprises would abandon such solutions because of vendor costs and hollowed-out internal skills. This is a reminder that FDE revenue design contains a long-term balance: **the value you create must remain greater than the cost and dependency created by your presence. Capture the spread on value and the business lasts; charge rent on dependency and it will eventually turn against you**.

## 6.6 Turn Penalties into Rewards: The Pricing Psychology of Usage and Expansion

Good mechanism design can turn punishment into reward. In the FDE business model, this insight applies to a subtle situation: **what should you do when customer usage exceeds expectations?**

The blunt approach is punitive overage pricing: once the contracted usage is exhausted, charge a punitive rate for the excess or throttle the system directly. This was common in the early days of cloud computing, with disastrous results. To avoid overages, customers deliberately suppress usage; utilization falls, value shrinks, and both sides lose at renewal. **You are punishing the very thing you most want: deep usage**.

The “turn penalties into rewards” approach redefines overage as a badge of growth. Three practical techniques follow.

Technique one: tiered pricing, with lower prices at higher usage. The more customers use, the lower the unit price. Turn overage anxiety into motivation to scale. The logic is the same as tiered data pricing in telecommunications, but the contract must make the story clear. The customer should see not “using more means paying more,” but “using more lowers the unit price and makes the economics better for both of us.” The bill is the same; the narrative changes, and so does the relationship.

Technique two: overage alerts plus proactive upgrades. When the system detects that a customer is about to exceed its allowance, do not quietly start billing. Go to the customer first: “Your usage is growing quickly. At this rate, moving up one tier will save you 15%.” Turn a billing event into a consultative-sales opportunity. The customer feels looked after rather than calculated against. There is also a hidden benefit: this forces your team to keep watching usage health, naturally connecting with the health framework in Chapter 5.

Technique three: give the customer credit for the savings. If usage optimization—model routing, caching, or batching—reduces costs, calculate the savings and show them to the customer: “This quarter, our architecture improvements saved you approximately X.” In the eyes of the customer’s finance leader, a vendor that proactively helps them save money is a different species from one waiting for them to exceed their allowance. The trust premium at renewal is worth far more than the fee saved.

The foundation of pricing psychology is simple: **your pricing structure tells customers every day what kind of relationship you believe you have**. A punitive structure says, “We are watching you.” A reward-based structure says, “We are growing together.” Your pricing model answers a question in the customer’s mind every day: whose side are you on? They will reread that answer before every renewal.

## 6.7 Build a Value Measurement System to Multiply Small Wins

The chapter closes with an infrastructure project: **build a value-measurement system that runs through every customer delivery**—turning “how much value did we create for the customer?” from an impression into data, and from data into an asset.

The ideas repeated throughout the earlier chapters meet inside this system. Chapter 2’s economic test is its input—the value hypothesis made at project inception. Chapter 4’s evaluation framework is its micro-foundation—quality data. Chapter 5’s health score is its operating interface—customer-relationship data. This chapter’s pricing and expansion are its commercial outlet—revenue data. Together, the system can do four things.

- **For customers—an evidence base for renewals and expansion:** Every quarterly review, renewal negotiation, and upgrade proposal is backed by a value report from the system: hours saved, error rates reduced, throughput increased, and the corresponding financial figures. Chapter 5 noted that value must be repeatedly reproven. This system is the production line for proof. **A data-backed renewal negotiation and a gut-feel renewal negotiation do not close at the same rate**.

- **For the company—a health check for delivery quality:** Aggregate value data across customers and you can answer questions that determine whether the model survives: Which use cases have the highest value density, and where should sales effort go? Which customers cost the most to deliver, and should pricing or the playbook change? Which deployments create value and which are spinning their wheels, and should resources be reallocated? Without this system, every answer is a guess.

- **For the product—an amplifier for feedback:** Value data combined with usage data provides the strongest basis for product decisions: which feature generates the most value and deserves more investment, which feature nobody uses and should be removed, and which scenario is repeatedly customized and therefore signals an opportunity to become a platform capability. This connects directly to Chapter 7: a value-measurement system is essentially the dashboard for the feedback pipeline from field to product.

- **For marketing—a library of trust-building material:** “Chemical usage reduced by 70%” and “investigation time reduced from hours to minutes”—the numbers that make an entire industry pay attention all come from accumulated value-measurement data. The highest form of case-study marketing is not storytelling but showing the numbers. Those numbers do not appear at the negotiating table by themselves; collection begins on the first day of delivery.

Three practical recommendations will help you build the system. First, collect a baseline from day one. Without pre-change data, there is no proof of post-change value, and the baseline exists only when the project starts; once missed, it is gone forever. Second, build the metrics with the customer. A metric the customer does not accept has no negotiating power, no matter how elegantly calculated. Once both sides agree at the kickoff meeting, the metric becomes your shared language. Third, keep the number of metrics under control. Three to five core metrics per customer are enough; once there are too many, there are effectively none.

That brings the revenue chapter to a close. From the customer-acquisition economics of free validation, to the pricing revolution of outcome-based models, to the expansion rhythm of deepening existing accounts, and finally to the infrastructure of value measurement, every thread points to the same conclusion: **FDE revenue is not sales’ trophy; it is the shadow cast by value. The greater the value you create, the longer the shadow**.
